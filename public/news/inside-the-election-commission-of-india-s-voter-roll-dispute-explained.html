Inside the Election Commission of India's Voter-Roll Dispute, Explained

Reported by A D Saurabh Filed on 2026-09-27 9 min read

A dispute over how India's election authority manages voter rolls broke into public view this week, with opposition parties calling for the Chief Election Commissioner to step down and the Commission issuing an unusually forceful same-day rebuttal. Here's what's actually being alleged, how the Commission has responded, and the background needed to understand why it matters.

  • 2. The core allegation
  • 3. The Commission's response
  • 4. What is the Special Intensive Revision, and why is it central here

1. What happened this week

On September 25, supporters of the opposition Congress party protested outside the Election Commission of India's New Delhi headquarters, demanding the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar. The protest followed newspaper reporting, most prominently in The Indian Express, describing repeated internal objections from the Commission's two other members over how voter-roll decisions had been handled.

The following day, the Commission ordered a review of the software system used to manage voter rolls, and said local election officials would now visit voters flagged for discrepancies at their homes rather than routinely requiring them to travel to government offices to resolve the issue.

2. The core allegation

According to the reporting that triggered the controversy, Election Commissioners Sukhbir Singh Sandhu and Vivek Joshi formally raised objections on at least fourteen occasions over roughly ten months. Their concerns reportedly centered on three areas: changes to voter registration procedures, the deletion and restoration of names on electoral rolls, and the centralization of the voter database into a system known as ECINET — all of which, the report claimed, were decided without adequately consulting the two commissioners beforehand.

Opposition parties have gone further, alleging that recent changes to voter lists — which excluded millions of voters in some areas — were skewed to benefit Prime Minister Narendra Modi's Bharatiya Janata Party. The Commission has rejected that characterization.

3. The Commission's response

The Election Commission pushed back directly, stating that disagreement during deliberation is normal for any institution and that every significant decision cited in the reporting — including the SIR order itself — had unanimous approval from all three commissioners. It pointed specifically to the original SIR order of June 24, 2025, which began in Bihar and West Bengal; a further rollout schedule for 12 states and union territories issued in late October 2025; and a third phase covering 19 more states and union territories issued in May 2026 — all of which it said were approved without dissent.

The Commission said all its actions were lawful, and framed the internal differences described in the reporting as a routine part of institutional decision-making rather than evidence of impropriety.

To address the software concerns directly, the Commission announced that a committee — led by a senior Deputy Election Commissioner and including an independent technology expert from an IIT or IIIT — would examine whether the ECINET system complies with election law and procedure.

4. What is the Special Intensive Revision, and why is it central here

The Special Intensive Revision, or SIR, is a nationwide, house-to-house re-verification of India's electoral rolls that the Commission began in mid-2025, starting with Bihar ahead of that state's assembly election. The exercise is meant to remove ineligible or duplicate entries and add eligible voters who were previously missing — but any large-scale roll revision inevitably removes some legitimate voters too, which is where most of the current controversy originates.

The Bihar phase became the subject of a Supreme Court challenge, with petitioners arguing that the accepted list of identity documents was too narrow and risked disenfranchising voters, particularly the poor and migrant workers. The Court intervened during the process to direct the Commission to accept documents such as Aadhaar cards as valid proof, and later upheld the legality of the SIR itself in a ruling delivered in May 2026, finding it consistent with the Commission's constitutional mandate to conduct free and fair elections. The revision has since been extended to dozens of additional states and union territories.

5. How ECI decisions actually get made

The Election Commission of India is a three-member body, and by design, no single member — including the Chief Election Commissioner — holds a veto. Decisions are meant to be made collectively, with a majority vote used if the three members don't reach agreement. That structure is precisely why the "unanimous approval" framing in the Commission's rebuttal is legally significant: if the disputed decisions really were unanimous, the objections described in the reporting would relate more to process and communication than to the legal validity of the outcomes.

It's also worth noting a change in how commissioners themselves are selected. A 2023 law altered the selection committee's composition, replacing the Chief Justice of India with a Union Cabinet minister nominated by the Prime Minister. Critics argue this shifted the balance of the appointment process toward the government of the day; the change has drawn recorded dissent from the Leader of the Opposition in at least one recent appointment.

6. What happens next

Two threads are now running in parallel: the political dispute over the Chief Election Commissioner's leadership, which opposition parties are likely to keep pressing, and the technical review of the ECINET software, which has a defined committee and a specific mandate to report on legal compliance. The findings of that review — and whether the Commission publishes them in detail — will likely shape how much of this dispute is remembered as a procedural disagreement versus something more consequential for how India's voter rolls are managed going forward.
